The ladder is the CEFR scale. A goal may stop at any rung: "everyday conversation" is B1, "fluent"
is usually C1. With no endpoint, plan to the next rung.

## Rungs, as outcomes the person can check

Milestones are these outcomes. Use the rung as the start of the milestone `key` (`b1-…`).

- **Rung — pre-A1** (only for a new script or unfamiliar sounds: Cyrillic, Greek, Arabic, tones).
  Read aloud and pronounce the script. For Chinese or Japanese characters it never finishes: plan
  it as the first stretch, then it goes on inside practice.
- **Rung — A1.** Introduce yourself and answer simple questions about yourself (name, home, work,
  family, numbers, prices, times). Then get through a whole service exchange: order, buy a ticket,
  ask and follow directions.
- **Rung — A2.** Hold a five-minute chat about your week, including last weekend, simply.
- **Rung — B1.** Follow the gist of a learner podcast at normal speed. Then hold an unrehearsed
  15-minute conversation about your day, work or plans, and handle something unexpected (a lost
  bag, a wrong bill) without switching to English.
- **Rung — B2.** Take part in a discussion at native speakers' pace, putting a view with its pros
  and cons; follow TV or a native podcast on familiar topics without subtitles; read an opinion
  piece in a newspaper.
- **Rung — C1.** Speak on complex subjects without searching for words; follow unstructured speech
  (a film, a fast debate); write a well-built piece of several pages; switch register between
  formal and casual.
- **Rung — C2.** Understand virtually everything heard or read; convey fine shades of meaning;
  recover from a slip so smoothly others hardly notice.

## Hours, by distance from English

Cumulative total study hours for an adult studying mostly alone. FSI is the US Foreign Service
Institute's ranking (class hours to professional level, about C1: ~600–750, ~900, ~1,100, ~2,200).
I is close to English (Spanish, French, Italian, Dutch, the Scandinavian languages); II is German;
III is most others (Bulgarian, Polish, Russian, Greek, Turkish, Hindi); IV is Mandarin, Japanese,
Korean, Arabic.

## Placement

Start at the rung already reached; a goal can begin anywhere on the ladder.

- **Up to B1:** the Council of Europe's CEFR self-assessment grid (free); DIALANG (free, 14
  European languages); a cultural institute's online placement test. Or one tutor session booked
  as a placement check: present tense, then past and future, then an opinion or a problem to
  solve. The level is where the talk breaks down.
- **B2 and up:** a past paper of the language's exam (DELE, DELF/DALF, Goethe, TestDaF, TORFL,
  HSK; JLPT tests no speaking or writing), or an oral proficiency interview (ACTFL OPI).
- Tests measure recognition more than speaking: start a rung below a test result, move up if easy.
- **Uneven skills are common past the novice stage:** rate listening, reading, speaking and writing
  separately and plan the weak one. A person who understands well but speaks poorly needs output,
  not more input. One who speaks well but writes poorly needs reading-to-write and reconstruction.
  A heritage speaker (grew up hearing it at home) is often fluent orally but weak at reading,
  writing and formal language: start at literacy and formal register, never a beginner course.

## Practice — by rung

The weekly time splits across four strands (Nation): input, output, study (grammar, vocabulary)
and fluency. Evidence strength is in brackets.

- **Practice — A1–A2:** about 40% study, 35% input, 15% output and 10% fluency. Spaced-repetition
  review, 10–15 new cards a day in 15–25 minutes (strong); more than ~25 a day and reviews pile up
  until the deck is dropped. Graded readers (strong). Dictation (A1–B1). An audio-first course
  for the first 4–8 weeks, for sounds and confidence (Pimsleur-style; ~250–500 words a level, no
  reading). Tutor every 1–2 weeks for pronunciation and speaking checks (common practice; no
  study settles the cadence).
- **Practice — B1:** about 15–20% study, 40% input, 25–30% output and 15% fluency. Grammar now
  follows the mistakes actually made. 4/3/2: the same talk in 4, then 3, then 2 minutes (strong,
  for fluency). Retelling what was heard or read (strong). Dictogloss: hear a short text twice,
  rebuild it, compare (strong, for grammar). Shadowing: speak along with a short native clip
  (moderate to strong for rhythm and listening, weak for accuracy). Sentence mining into the review
  deck (moderate). Tutor once or twice a week; ask to be prompted to self-correct, not only
  corrected (prompts beat recasts). Conversation exchange starts to work here.
- **Practice — B2–C1:** mostly authentic input and pushed output. Extensive reading, with about a
  million words a year as the target for mid-frequency vocabulary (strong). Narrow reading or
  listening: one topic or one author at a time (moderate to strong). Weekly writing of 400–600
  words that the tutor edits. An error log reviewed before each session (moderate to strong, for
  writing). Imitating model texts for a genre (strong). The tutor becomes editor and sparring
  partner. Vocabulary moves to collocations and register; unassisted novels need ~8,000–9,000
  word families.
- **Practice — maintain (any rung):** a light upkeep keeps a level (a fortnightly conversation,
  regular reading or listening, a short review). Most is lost in the first few years of disuse.
  The higher the level reached, the more stays for decades (Bahrick).
- **Not supported:** background listening without attention, sleep learning, matching a "learning
  style", an app alone past A2, input only past B1, speed reading.

## Tracks — branches off the ladder

Each opens at its entry rung, alongside the main ladder or after it.

- **Track — specialised vocabulary** (a profession or field). From B1–B2 with knowledge of the
  field. Technical words are 20–40% of a field's texts. Outcomes: read a field document without a
  dictionary; write a memo or report; follow a meeting. ~150–250 hours for ~1,500 terms (one
  estimate). Narrow reading in the field, terms reviewed with their collocations, terminology
  databases.
- **Track — reading literature.** Genre fiction from high B2; literary fiction C1; classics and
  poetry C2. Outcomes: finish a crime novel without fatigue, then a literary one. Volume reading,
  one author at a time, an e-reader dictionary.
- **Track — academic or professional writing.** From B2. Outcomes: a report colleagues use
  unedited; an abstract or paper accepted. Genre analysis of published examples, phrasebanks,
  the Academic Word List (570 word families, ~10% of academic text). ~200–300 hours (one estimate).
- **Track — creative writing** (to move to its own playbook when one serving native writers
  exists). From strong B2. Outcomes: a 1,500-word story with controlled past-tense narration (B2);
  figurative language not carried over from English (C1); voice and irony (C2). Model-text
  imitation, writing under constraints, a free draft then an editing pass; feedback from
  native-speaking writers, not only correction.
- **Track — translation.** Into one's own language from high B2–C1; into the learned language
  C1–C2. Professional competence takes 1–2 years of training beyond C1. Compare with professional
  translations; post-edit machine translation.
- **Track — a proficiency exam.** Only once at the exam's level: 6–10 weeks of timed past papers
  and the marking rubric on top. Preparing below the level is wasted.

## Graduation — options for the conversation about what's next

Reaching a rung is noted. When the person talks about what's next, these are the options to offer.
"Stop here and maintain" comes first when they set this rung as the goal.

- **A2:** on to B1, or maintain (enough for travel).
- **B1:** on to B2, a specialised-vocabulary track, or maintain.
- **B2:** on to C1, or the literature, professional-writing or exam track.
- **C1 and up:** C2, creative writing or translation. Being at C1 is itself the best protection
  against loss.

## Planning notes

- The recurring practice (daily review, listening, the weekly class) is the person's weekly time,
  never a step. A milestone's `minutes` is its whole estimate: the hours between its rung and the
  previous one, for the language's row, split across that rung's outcomes. Pace it at their
  weekly time.
- Steps are one-off things that move them to the next outcome. Examples: take a placement check;
  choose a course and set up a review deck; book a trial tutor session; finish a unit; record a
  4/3/2 talk and go through it with the tutor; a first real conversation with a stranger; sit a
  past paper.
- End each milestone with a step that checks its outcome. The check is the evidence, not the hours.
- When the person's tutor runs a syllabus, plan milestones around it, not beside it.
- What they want the language for decides which topics and track come first.

## Resources, by type

As researched 2026-11-24; examples date fast. None is required.

1. **Spaced-repetition apps:** Anki (free; $25 one-off on iOS), Memrise, Clozemaster.
2. **Audio courses** (A0–A2): Pimsleur (~$15–21 a month), Michel Thomas, Language Transfer (free).
3. **Tutors** (italki, Preply): community tutors ~$8–18 an hour; qualified teachers ~$18–40+.
4. **Conversation exchange** (B1+): Tandem, HelloTalk. Free core; partners rarely correct.
5. **Reading:** graded readers (A2–B2), LingQ, Readlang, then authentic books.
6. **Listening:** learner podcasts (A2–B1), then native media. If more than one word in five is
   a guess, it is too hard to learn from.
7. **AI-made material\*:** vocabulary lists and dialogues at a level, graded texts built on chosen
   grammar, quizzes, glossaries before a chapter, text practice. It is weak at correcting errors
   (too agreeable), in less widely spoken languages, at register and niche collocations, and it
   gives no pronunciation feedback. Its output needs a tutor's eye.

\* Metatron can write a list or a dialogue when asked in conversation. It cannot yet make, save or
send material on its own (no review deck, no scheduled drills), so plan no step that depends on it
doing so; "ask Metatron for this week's words" is a step the person takes.

## Common failure points

1. The plateaus, at A2→B1 and again at B2→C1: progress stops showing, "good enough" sets in and
   errors set. What helps is more speaking under some pressure with feedback, comparing one's own
   recording with a corrected version, narrow input, and 4/3/2.
2. No speaking practice: an app-only learner recognises plenty and can say little.
3. Too much at once: too many new cards, several courses together.
4. Native media too early, before learner material is used up.
5. Planning to the average when one skill lags.
6. Stopping cold after reaching the goal instead of a light upkeep.
7. Placing too high from a recognition test, or preparing for an exam below its level.
